Good morning, colleagues. This is the monthly anesthesiology bulletin from Boletines Médicos, covering September twentieth to October twentieth, twenty twenty-six. The month was shaped by the annual meeting of the American Society of Anesthesiologists, held in San Diego from October sixteenth to twentieth. Keep one thing in mind: most meeting presentations are abstracts that have not yet undergone full peer review, and several of today's studies are observational.

We begin with a negative result worth noting. In Shenyang, China, a single-center, three-arm randomized trial with patient and assessor blinding enrolled one hundred eighty-six patients aged sixty to eighty having laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery. It compared induction with two doses of remimazolam against propofol. Delirium within five days occurred in twelve point nine and eleven point three percent with remimazolam, versus six point five percent with propofol, with no significant difference. Remimazolam did bring less treated hypotension, less bradycardia, and lower vasopressor use. A caution: the trial was underpowered, because it assumed thirty-three percent delirium with propofol and observed far less, so it neither proves nor rules out an effect on delirium.

In regional anesthesia, a meta-analysis from Seoul National University Bundang Hospital pooled eight randomized trials and six hundred eighty-four adults having knee surgery. Adding a popliteal plexus block to another block reduced pain at twelve and twenty-four hours and sharply reduced nausea and vomiting. Evidence for opioid sparing is weak, with very heterogeneous studies, and the result becomes borderline when a single study is excluded.

Turning to perioperative medicine, a team at the University of Texas in Houston analyzed records from just over two million adults having elective surgery. About one hundred forty-five thousand were taking glucagon-like peptide one receptor agonists. Compared with matched nonusers, the odds of major cardiovascular complications were eleven percent lower, and the odds of postoperative infections eight percent lower. The caveat is clear: this is observational, prone to healthy-user bias, and it says nothing about pulmonary aspiration, our main concern in the operating room.

Next, a simple intervention. At the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, in China, one hundred ninety-two patients having minimally invasive gynecologic surgery were randomized to noise-canceling headphones or none. Nausea and vomiting within forty-eight hours fell from fifty-two point one to thirty-five point four percent, with no increase in adverse events. It is cheap and safe, but it comes from a single center, blinding is impossible, and it needs replication.

Investigators from the University of South Florida and Moffitt Cancer Center studied two hundred fifty-eight patients who had tumor resection, sixty-four of whom developed delirium, in a retrospective matched case-control design. Those who combined walking with moderate or vigorous activity at least seven times a week had half the risk of postoperative delirium compared with less active patients. Activity was self-reported, and more active patients also tend to be healthier.

On postoperative follow-up, researchers at the University of Toronto analyzed thirty-eight studies with seven thousand five hundred seven patients. App-based or web-based monitoring was linked to a twenty-two percent lower risk of major complications and of emergency visits, although total complications did not fall significantly. The key detail: readmissions dropped only when a clinician reviewed the data. Technology alone is not enough.

In obstetric anesthesia, a meta-analysis of six randomized trials and one thousand eight hundred thirty-two women having elective cesarean delivery found that ketamine or esketamine, during or after surgery, was linked to fewer depressive symptoms between days three and fourteen. The effect was small to moderate. A screening score is not a diagnosis, and the authors say it is too early to claim that ketamine prevents postpartum depression.

For sedation outside the operating room, a single-center records review of two hundred eighty endoscopic procedures found that patients who smoked or vaped cannabis received a mean initial propofol dose of about one hundred ninety-three milligrams in upper endoscopy, versus about one hundred fifty-one in nonusers. There were no meaningful differences in colonoscopy. The user group was small, so the lesson is to ask about route of use, not to change dosing.

Finally, sustainability. A six-site academic health system in Virginia reviewed nearly one hundred forty-six thousand anesthetics. By cutting desflurane, lowering fresh gas flows, and giving each anesthesiologist feedback on emissions, emissions per case fell forty-five percent and inhaled agent spending fell almost forty percent, with no worse patient outcomes. It is a before-and-after study, but the practical message applies to any operating room.
