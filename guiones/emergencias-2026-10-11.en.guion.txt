Good morning, colleagues. This is the emergency and critical care medicine bulletin from Boletines Médicos, for October eleventh, twenty twenty-six. It has been a short period, just thirteen days, and a quiet one for large trials. So we also bring you the highlights of the European Emergency Medicine Congress, held in Paris in late September. In all, we cover eight stories.

We start with a practical alert for the intensive care unit. On September twenty-ninth, the United States Food and Drug Administration classified as a Class One recall, its most serious category, the problem affecting certain infusion sets for Alaris pumps made by Becton Dickinson, and it updated the notice on October fifth. With some components, such as filters, valves or microbore tubing, the pump is less accurate than claimed, especially below one milliliter per hour and with boluses of five milliliters or less. The risk is over or under infusion, with delayed occlusion alarms. Critically ill patients, newborns and children are most exposed. No serious injuries or deaths had been reported as of August nineteenth. If your hospital uses these pumps, check your inventory and favor syringe pumps for low-rate vasopressors and sedatives. Separately, the agency also classified as a Class One recall the Draeger VentStar Resus Neo breathing circuits, because of hose cracks that can cause leaks or disconnection, with a risk of hypoxia.

Next, toxicology, with a randomized trial from India on Russell's viper bites. Two antivenom regimens with the same loading dose were compared. One was short, thirty-six hours, with higher maintenance doses. The other was long, seventy-two hours. One hundred ninety-three patients were randomized, and one hundred sixty-two completed the protocol. Mortality was eight point five percent with the short regimen and seventeen point two percent with the long one, a difference that was not statistically significant. Among patients who completed the protocol, the short regimen brought faster recovery, less acute kidney injury, twenty-three versus fifty-one percent, and less shock. The caveat: a single center, a primary outcome that was not significant, and a snake and antivenom different from those in our region.

From the Netherlands comes an idea to relieve crowded departments. In the Rotterdam region, ambulance crews measured high-sensitivity troponin at the patient's side for chest pain and completed a standard risk score. With this strategy, thirty-four percent of chest pain patients stayed home, compared with three point five percent before. Among them, only one in one hundred sixty-five had a major cardiac event within a month. The caveat: it was not randomized, these are preliminary congress data, and follow-up was complete in only eighty-six percent of patients.

On crowding itself, a United Kingdom study of more than nineteen thousand patients in one hundred thirty-four departments found that every ten percent rise in occupancy was associated with one percent higher mortality at twenty-eight days. Average occupancy was one hundred seventy-five percent. The caveat: this is an association, not proof of cause, and the uncertainty is wide.

In resuscitation, a French group simulated drone delivery of defibrillators around Paris, using more than twenty-eight thousand cardiac arrests. Combining drones with fixed devices, more than ninety-five percent of patients would have a defibrillator within five minutes. The caveat: it is a theoretical model, with no real drones and no survival data.

In sepsis, the Food and Drug Administration granted breakthrough device status to a selective cell-filtering therapy from SeaStar Medical, used within continuous kidney replacement therapy. The caveat: a designation is not an approval, and the supporting data are pediatric and compared with historical controls.

On staff safety, a European survey of more than one thousand three hundred clinicians in eighty-one countries found that three in four witnessed workplace violence in the past year, and only half had prevention training. The caveat: a voluntary sample, with about forty percent of responses excluded.

And in artificial intelligence, researchers in Saudi Arabia tested a large language model for triage in a simulated pilgrimage setting. Using synthetic dialogues, it was correct seventy-one percent of the time. The caveat: the dialogues were generated with knowledge of the right answer, and there was no testing with real patients.

On the calendar, the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine meets in Lisbon from October tenth to fourteenth, with scientific sessions from the twelfth. The Japanese Association for Acute Medicine meets in Nagasaki from October twenty-seventh to twenty-ninth.
