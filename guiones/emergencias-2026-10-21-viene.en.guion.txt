This edition of what's next in emergency medicine looks ahead five months, from November two thousand twenty-six to March two thousand twenty-seven. We bring three milestones with verifiable dates: one trial to be presented as late-breaking science at the American Heart Association Scientific Sessions in Chicago, and two pending decisions by the United States Food and Drug Administration on acute pain and anaphylaxis. These are not results yet. They are dates on the calendar, and it pays to arrive with the clinical question in mind.

First, the RACE-CARS trial on out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. On Sunday, November eighth, the primary results of this open-label cluster randomized trial across fifty North Carolina counties, led by Duke University with the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute as collaborator, will be presented. It compares usual care with a package of rapid arrest recognition by nine one one call-takers, telephone-guided bystander resuscitation, community training in resuscitation and defibrillator use, and better first responder performance. The primary outcome is survival with good neurologic function at discharge, in roughly twenty thousand patients estimated. The problem is familiar: the chain of survival breaks before the patient reaches us. Critical take: with so many components, even a positive result will not tell us which one mattered, and transfer depends on the maturity of each dispatch system.

Second, Viatris's fast-acting meloxicam for moderate to severe acute pain. The agency's target action date is December twenty-seventh, two thousand twenty-six. The application rests on two randomized, double-blind phase three trials, after hernia repair and bunion surgery, with nearly one thousand randomized patients in total, against placebo with tramadol as an active control; the primary outcome was summed pain intensity difference over the first forty-eight hours. The company presents it as a potential first-line oral non-opioid option. Critical take: these are postoperative pain models, not emergency department patients, the company's release gives no efficacy or safety figures, and the renal, gastrointestinal and cardiovascular risks of an anti-inflammatory remain.

Third, Anaphylm, a sublingual film carrying an epinephrine prodrug for type one allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis. After a complete response letter in January, the company resubmitted in September with new human factors and pharmacokinetic studies, and the new target action date is March sixteenth, two thousand twenty-seven. The need is real: many patients do not carry or do not use their autoinjector. Critical take: approval would rest on plasma levels, not clinical outcomes in anaphylaxis, absorption with throat swelling or vomiting remains an open question, and it does not replace intramuscular epinephrine in the emergency department.

In short: one confirmed date in November for cardiac arrest, and two regulatory decisions in December and March. We will return to each when the data arrive.
