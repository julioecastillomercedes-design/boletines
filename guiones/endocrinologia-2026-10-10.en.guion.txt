Good morning, colleagues. This is the Boletines Médicos endocrinology and diabetes bulletin for October tenth, twenty twenty-six. It covers September twenty-eighth to October tenth, a period shaped by the European diabetes congress in Milan. We review nine verified stories.

First story. The New England Journal of Medicine published on October first the Synchronize two trial of survodutide, a dual glucagon and glucagon-like peptide one agonist, in seven hundred fifty-five adults with obesity or overweight and type two diabetes over seventy-six weeks. With the treatment-regimen analysis, weight fell nine point eight percent with the high dose, versus three point nine percent with placebo; assuming full adherence, up to thirteen point one percent. Glycated hemoglobin fell by less than one point. Critical reading: the nine point eight percent figure is the one to quote, and one in four participants stopped because of adverse events, mostly digestive.

Second story. The Achieve four trial, published in The Lancet on September thirtieth, compared oral orforglipron with insulin glargine in two thousand seven hundred forty-nine adults with type two diabetes and established cardiovascular or kidney disease. The oral drug was noninferior for major cardiovascular events, with a hazard ratio of zero point eight four. According to the company, at one year weight fell almost nine percent with orforglipron while it rose slightly with glargine. Critical reading: it is an open-label trial with a wide margin and no superiority, and the lower mortality is only exploratory.

Third story. In a phase two trial of three hundred sixty-seven adults with type two diabetes, presented by the company on September thirtieth, the combination of eloralintide, an amylin agonist, with tirzepatide achieved up to twenty-three point three percent weight loss at forty-eight weeks, versus fourteen point eight percent with tirzepatide alone. Critical reading: that comparison was secondary, these are company data without peer-reviewed publication, and up to twenty-seven percent stopped because of adverse events on the combination.

Fourth story. In a six-month randomized trial of one hundred forty-three people with poorly controlled type one diabetes, a smart insulin pen connected to continuous glucose monitoring was noninferior to injections plus monitoring. Those who responded more often to its alerts had eight more points of time in range. Critical reading: that analysis compares more and less adherent patients, it is not a randomized comparison, and it was presented by the manufacturer.

Fifth story, without drugs. The Reset for Remission trial, run in Canada and England, gave ninety-six young adults with early-onset type two diabetes a diet of eight hundred to nine hundred calories a day for twelve weeks, with supervised exercise. At twenty-four weeks, fifty-four percent achieved remission, versus four percent with usual care. Critical reading: it is small and short, and we do not know how long remission lasts, but it is cheap and transferable to our setting.

Sixth story. The Lancet published on September twenty-ninth the Triumph two trial of retatrutide in one thousand one hundred fifty-two adults with obesity and type two diabetes over eighty weeks. With twelve milligrams, weight fell eighteen point eight percent, versus five point one percent with placebo. Critical reading: quote that figure rather than the twenty point eight percent in the press release, and watch for low blood pressure and altered skin sensation.

Seventh story, from South Korea. According to the Korean pharmaceutical press, the country's drug agency approved on October seventh efpeglenatide, from Hanmi, the first glucagon-like peptide one agonist developed in Korea for obesity. In a trial of four hundred forty-eight Korean adults, weight fell on average nine point seven five percent at forty weeks. Critical reading: modest efficacy, interim data, and a Korean population only.

Eighth story. The Lancet Diabetes and Endocrinology published the phase two Zupreme one trial of weekly petrelintide in four hundred ninety-three adults with obesity without diabetes. Weight loss reached nine point eight percent at twenty-eight weeks, and ten point seven percent at forty-two weeks in an exploratory analysis. Only one percent stopped because of digestive complaints. Critical reading: its strength is tolerability, not the size of the effect.

Ninth story, in thyroid disease. The United States drug agency approved on September twenty-eighth tiratricol, from Egetis, the first treatment for peripheral thyrotoxicosis in deficiency of the M C T eight transporter, or Allan Herndon Dudley syndrome, in adults and children. The evidence comes from a withdrawal trial with fifteen randomized patients and an open study with forty-six. Critical reading: the evidence is small, and the drug falsely raises triiodothyronine results in laboratory immunoassays.
