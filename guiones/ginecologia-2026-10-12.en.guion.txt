Good morning, colleagues. This is the obstetrics and gynecology newscast, covering what was published between October second and October twelfth, twenty twenty-six. We bring you seven verified stories.

We open with the story of widest reach. On October seventh, the British Medical Journal published a case-control study based on Swedish national registers on the quadrivalent human papillomavirus vaccine given before pregnancy. It analyzed six hundred twenty-four thousand seven hundred thirteen singleton births to women with no previous births, from two thousand six to two thousand twenty-three, and almost fifteen percent of them had been vaccinated. After adjustment, vaccinated women had about five percent lower odds of preterm birth and about fifteen percent lower odds of very preterm birth, between twenty-eight and thirty-one weeks. The benefit was generally greater when the vaccine was given before age seventeen. The critical reading: this is an observational study, the effect is small, and residual confounding cannot be ruled out, but it adds an obstetric argument for vaccinating early.

Next, the Dominican Republic. According to the latest bulletin of the Directorate of Epidemiology, as reported by Diario Libre on October eighth, the country has recorded one hundred twelve maternal deaths through epidemiological week thirty-eight. Fifty-eight were Haitian women, fifty-two were Dominican women, and two were of other nationalities. Infant deaths total one thousand four hundred forty-eight, and eighty-four percent occurred before twenty-eight days of life. The data are preliminary and the report does not give causes. In addition, the figure does not square with the count of ninety-eight through week thirty-five that we published in September, which suggests late notification or reclassification, so the reduction compared with last year should be read with caution.

Now to gynecologic oncology. On October seventh, the surgery journal of the American Medical Association published an analysis of secondary outcomes of the European trial known as rack, coordinated from the Karolinska Institute, comparing robot-assisted with open radical hysterectomy in early cervical cancer. Among nine hundred randomized patients, complications during surgery occurred in five point seven percent with the robot, against ten percent with open surgery, mostly less serious events. At thirty days, complications were similar, thirty-three point four against thirty-four point eight percent. The warning comes from the authors themselves: there is no survival result yet, and these data cannot be used to judge the oncologic safety of the robotic route.

In menopause, the American College of Physicians announced on October fifth its guideline on drug treatment of vasomotor symptoms. First line is estrogen with a progestogen for women with a uterus, and estrogen alone for women without one. Second line, when estrogen is contraindicated or not tolerated, is venlafaxine or desvenlafaxine. Third line includes escitalopram, paroxetine, gabapentin, and the neurokinin antagonists fezolinetant and elinzanetant. The critical reading: the guideline places the new and costly drugs on the last rung, which supports those who practice with limited resources.

More data arrived on those same drugs. At the world congress of the International Menopause Society, a preliminary analysis of an observational real-world study of fezolinetant was presented, and the manufacturer publicized it on October second. At twelve weeks, women had two point six fewer nighttime awakenings, measured with a wearable motion sensor, and about nine fewer minutes awake during the night. The data come from the manufacturer, Astellas, the study has no comparison group, it measures only change from baseline, and it has no peer-reviewed publication yet.

In contraception, a double-blind randomized trial from Mount Sinai in New York, published on October eighth in the American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology, tested transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation for pain during intrauterine device placement. Ninety-nine women took part. Mean pain was fifty point eight millimeters with active stimulation, and fifty point six with sham stimulation. There was no significant difference. It is a negative and useful result: the equipment is not worth buying for this purpose.

We close with the pelvic floor. A group at Seoul National University compared nonabsorbable sutures with delayed-absorbable sutures in vaginal uterosacral ligament suspension. There were three hundred ten patients. At one year, surgical failure was thirteen point seven percent with nonabsorbable suture, against thirty-eight point one percent with delayed-absorbable suture. In exchange, permanent suture had more complications requiring intervention, five point nine against one percent. The study is retrospective, from a single surgeon, with groups operated on in different periods, so it guides the discussion without closing it.
