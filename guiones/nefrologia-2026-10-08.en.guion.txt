Good morning, colleagues. This is the nephrology bulletin covering September eighth to October eighth, twenty twenty-six. The month was dominated by regulatory decisions in glomerular disease. Here are twelve verified items, ordered by clinical importance; two of them are slightly older than the period, and we say so.

We open with immunoglobulin A nephropathy. The final analysis of the randomized, double-blind Origin three trial, with four hundred twenty-eight patients, showed that atacicept nearly halted filtration decline over two years. The slope was minus zero point six milliliters per minute per year, versus minus five point six with placebo. The risk of kidney progression fell seventy-six percent. The critical read: this could convert the July accelerated approval into full approval, but these are still press-release data without peer review.

On September sixteenth, Bayer announced that the United States Food and Drug Administration approved finerenone for chronic kidney disease associated with type one diabetes. In the randomized, double-blind Fine One trial of two hundred forty-two adults, albuminuria fell twenty-eight percent more than with placebo at six months. Hyperkalemia occurred in ten point one percent versus three point three percent. The critical read: according to the company, this is the first new treatment in thirty years for this population, but it rests on a surrogate endpoint without hard kidney outcomes.

On September twenty-fifth, the same agency approved obinutuzumab to reduce the risk of relapse in childhood-onset idiopathic nephrotic syndrome. In the open-label phase three Inshore trial of eighty-five patients, sustained complete remission at one year was ninety-five point five percent, versus seventy-three point two percent with mycophenolate mofetil. The critical read: this is the first new therapy in decades for this disease, but the trial is small and open-label, and the cost is high.

In Japan, on September sixteenth, iptacopan, an oral complement factor B inhibitor, was approved for adult immunoglobulin A nephropathy. In the Applause trial, it reduced the risk of a composite endpoint of progression to kidney failure by forty-three percent and slowed filtration decline by forty-nine point three percent. The critical read: Japan has one of the highest prevalences of this disease, and the final two-year results are already published in the New England Journal of Medicine.

Turning to transplantation, on September third, five days before this issue's period, the first case of a gene-edited pig kidney used as a bridge to human transplant was published. A sixty-six-year-old man lived two hundred seventy-one days off dialysis before the xenograft failed. He then received a deceased-donor kidney with immediate function. The critical read: it is a single case, but xenotransplantation can serve as a bridge.

A target trial emulation compared deceased-donor transplantation with remaining on dialysis in patients aged seventy-five or older. Recipients had higher early mortality, but a survival advantage emerged after about three years. The critical read: the design is observational, but it supports not excluding patients from the waitlist on age alone.

At an international transplantation congress in Sydney, the extension of the Bestow study was presented. At two years, with eighty-one of one hundred twenty enrolled patients, mean filtration was seventy-one with tegoprubart versus fifty-eight with tacrolimus. The critical read: these are manufacturer data from a phase two trial with patient attrition, and practice should not change until the Legacy trial confirms non-inferiority.

Back to chronic kidney disease, a cross-sectional Brazilian study published in January, outside this period, included more than fourteen thousand people with diabetes or hypertension. Fewer than five percent had albuminuria ordered before a national campaign, and fewer than seven percent afterward. The critical read: without albuminuria there is no early diagnosis, in Brazil or in the Dominican Republic.

A survey of sixty-one nephrology providers in the United States named cost as the top barrier to cardiorenal drugs. Cost was cited by eighty-four percent for glucagon-like peptide one receptor agonists. The critical read: the sample is small, but the gap is largely economic, and larger in our setting.

News reports describe young, healthy men, largely undocumented construction workers from Mexico and Central America in Houston, arriving with kidney failure without diabetes or hypertension. About one in six emergency dialysis cases among uninsured or undocumented patients had no identifiable cause. The critical read: this is journalism, but ask about occupation and heat in young patients with unexplained kidney disease.

In dialysis it was a quiet month, with no verifiable randomized trials. The Voice trial, comparing vadadustat with an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent in hemodialysis, will be presented on October twenty-third. In June, the companies announced a favorable interim safety analysis, without figures we could verify. The critical read: for now it is only an announcement.

Finally, an artificial intelligence virtual nephrologist simulates a consultation about transplantation. After using it, patients reported greater willingness to discuss transplantation with their doctor. The critical read: the outcome is self-reported with no control group.

On the calendar, the American Society of Nephrology Kidney Week takes place in Denver from October twenty-first to twenty-fifth. The Voice trial will be presented there, and we will review it next month. Thank you for listening. Until next month.
