Good morning, colleagues. This is the monthly pulmonology bulletin from Boletines Médicos, for October ninth, twenty twenty-six. We review the most relevant news of recent weeks, with a critical reading of each item. The month was shaped by the European Respiratory Society Congress, held in Barcelona in early September.

We begin with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Two phase three trials, called Oberon and Titania, tested tozorakimab, an antibody that blocks interleukin thirty-three. They enrolled two thousand three hundred six patients with frequent exacerbations, treated every four weeks for a year. In former smokers, moderate or severe exacerbations fell by twenty-nine percent in one trial and thirty-four percent in the other. Notably, in the pooled analysis of both trials, there was benefit even in patients with low eosinophil counts, with a twenty-three percent reduction. The results appeared in the New England Journal of Medicine, and a decision from the United States Food and Drug Administration is expected in early twenty twenty-seven. Critical take: a promising result, but manufacturer-funded and not yet approved.

Next, asthma. The phase two b Aircules trial, with full data presented at the European congress, tested lunsekimig, a single molecule that blocks both thymic stromal lymphopoietin and interleukin thirteen. According to published coverage, compared with placebo it cut exacerbations by about thirty-nine percent overall, and by about fifty-five percent at the highest dose. In patients with more than three prior exacerbations, the reduction approached eighty percent. Critical take: the benefit sits mainly in type two inflammation, where current biologics already work, and phase three data are still needed.

Now to pulmonary hypertension, the busiest area this month. On September twenty-second, the Food and Drug Administration expanded the sotatercept label with data from the Hyperion trial. The trial enrolled three hundred twenty adults with pulmonary arterial hypertension diagnosed within the previous year. Death or clinical worsening occurred in ten point six percent with sotatercept, versus thirty-six point nine percent with placebo. That is a seventy-six percent relative risk reduction. Critical take: the trial stopped early, which tends to inflate the effect, and cost will limit access in many countries.

Along the same lines, the European Respiratory Society published an update of its treatment guideline for pulmonary arterial hypertension on September seventh. It strongly recommends sotatercept as add-on therapy for patients who do not reach low risk despite treatment. It makes no recommendation for low-risk patients, given low certainty of evidence. Critical take: this updates, rather than replaces, the twenty twenty-two guideline, and the full text deserves a careful read.

Next, pulmonary hypertension linked to interstitial lung disease. The phase two Phocus trial tested mosliciguat, an inhaled activator of soluble guanylate cyclase taken once a day. It enrolled one hundred thirty-five patients in twenty countries. At sixteen weeks, pulmonary vascular resistance fell by fifty-one percent with the drug, while it rose by six point six percent with placebo. Six-minute walk distance, a secondary endpoint, also improved. A phase three trial with about three hundred seventy-five patients is now enrolling. Critical take: the primary endpoint is hemodynamic, not clinical, and the data so far come only from a company press release.

Turning to pulmonary nodules. At the World Conference on Lung Cancer in Seoul, a cancer center in Florida reported its experience with an artificial intelligence system linked to the health record. The system detects and classifies nodules on chest imaging. Over three years, it reviewed nearly three hundred forty-nine thousand studies and led to forty-nine lung cancer diagnoses. The share found at an early stage rose from fifty-three percent in twenty twenty-four to seventy-three percent in twenty twenty-six. The center's classic low-dose screening program found thirty-two cancers. Critical take: these are two different groups at a single center, without randomization or publication, so the finding is only a hypothesis.

In infections, sleep and ventilation, we found no verifiable news within the period. There were also no new verifiable data from the Dominican Republic or the rest of Latin America. We would rather say so than fill the space with old news.

A note on timing. Three of today's items were presented on September seventh and eighth, a day or two before our coverage window. We include them because of their clinical weight, and they are flagged in the written edition.
