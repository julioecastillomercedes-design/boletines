Good morning, colleagues. In this edition we look at what is coming in neurology between mid November twenty twenty-six and the first half of twenty twenty-seven: one Alzheimer trial due to report, three dated regulatory decisions in the United States, and a fourth with no announced date yet.

First, the Polaris trial of AR one thousand one, an oral phosphodiesterase five inhibitor, in early Alzheimer disease with confirmed amyloid. This randomized, double-blind phase three trial enrolled one thousand five hundred thirty-five patients with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia in thirteen countries, compared thirty milligrams daily with placebo over fifty-two weeks, and its primary outcome is the clinical dementia rating sum of boxes. The company says results will be presented at the Clinical Trials on Alzheimer's Disease meeting in Boston, November sixteenth to nineteenth. Today's anti-amyloid antibodies require infusions and safety imaging; a pill without imaging surveillance would broaden access. Caveats: the trial is manufacturer-funded, the mechanism does not target amyloid directly, and the result may well be negative.

Second, deramiocel, a cardiosphere-derived cell therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy. The United States Food and Drug Administration has a decision date of November twenty-second, moved from August twenty-second after the company submitted twenty-four-month extension data and an indication narrowed to upper limb function. In the phase three trial, with one hundred six patients randomized, the manufacturer reports a fifty-four percent slowing on the upper limb function scale versus placebo, with borderline statistical significance. The need is real: cardiomyopathy and loss of arm function in non-ambulant patients have few options beyond steroids and cardiac care. But on July twenty-ninth an advisory committee voted nine to three that there is no substantial evidence of effectiveness in cardiomyopathy, and the agency questioned late changes to the statistical analysis plan and the integrity of blinding.

Third, relutrigine, a preferential blocker of the persistent sodium current, for developmental and epileptic encephalopathies caused by variants in the SCN two A and SCN eight A genes. The decision date moved to December twenty-seventh after the company submitted additional analyses, with no new studies requested. In the phase two Embold study, in children aged two to eighteen, the confirmatory cohort reduced motor seizures by fifty-three percent placebo-adjusted at sixteen weeks, according to the company. Today these children receive combinations of antiseizure drugs with inconsistent response. The critical view: few patients, short follow-up, sponsor funding, and it requires genetic diagnosis, which is not always available.

Fourth, satralizumab, the interleukin six receptor antibody already approved for neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, now for myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease. It has priority review and, according to Roche, a decision is expected by January tenth, twenty twenty-seven. In the Meteoroid trial it cut relapse risk by sixty-eight percent versus placebo, and at forty-eight weeks eighty-seven percent versus sixty-seven percent were relapse-free. No drug is currently approved for this disease; steroids, immunoglobulin and rituximab are used off label. The key open question is whom to treat, since many patients have a single attack.

Fifth, fenebrutinib, an oral reversible Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor, accepted under priority review for relapsing and primary progressive multiple sclerosis, as Roche announced on September thirtieth. Roche has not given a date; on the six-month priority review timeline, a decision would fall in the first half of twenty twenty-seven. Against teriflunomide it reduced the annual relapse rate by fifty-one and fifty-eight percent in two trials. Against ocrelizumab in primary progressive disease the hazard ratio for progression was zero point eight eight, without demonstrated superiority.
