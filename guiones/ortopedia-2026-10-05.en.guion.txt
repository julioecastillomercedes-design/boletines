Good morning, colleagues. This bulletin covers September fifth to October fifth, twenty twenty-six. It was a light month for large trials: we include ten verified items rather than padding. Three come from outside North America and Western Europe: Australia, Taiwan and Japan. In Latin America and the Caribbean we found no verifiable clinical publications from the period.

The most important item comes from the United Kingdom's National Joint Registry, in two analyses published in the British Medical Journal on September thirtieth. They included six hundred sixty-six thousand two hundred eighty-three hip and six hundred ninety-seven thousand one hundred forty-five knee arthroplasties. With a mean follow-up of two point five years, robotic surgery did not reduce overall revision risk. The one exception was less revision for malposition in hips. It is observational and short-term, but the fewer revisions argument does not hold in the short term.

In hip fracture, a single-center Norwegian cohort analyzed four thousand one hundred thirty-six cases. At three months, the primary endpoint, mortality was numerically higher with delays beyond twelve hours, without statistical significance. At one year, waiting more than forty-eight hours was associated with an adjusted odds ratio of one point eight zero. The authors themselves urge caution: it is observational and contrasts with the Hip Attack trial.

In Australia, a randomized trial in Annals of Internal Medicine enrolled one hundred eighty-four adults with persistent knee problems after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction. Four months of progressive physiotherapist-supervised exercise improved symptoms and muscle strength versus a single education session. There was no cartilage deterioration. Follow-up is short, and prevention of early osteoarthritis is not yet shown.

In Taiwan, a retrospective cohort of six thousand one hundred seventy-one operations revealed a serious osteoporosis treatment gap. Within one year, treatment was started in forty-six point nine percent after vertebroplasty, but only seven point two percent after total knee arthroplasty. The gap persisted among patients with osteoporotic bone density. A preoperative bone health protocol is applicable in the Dominican Republic.

A Swedish randomized trial in selected discogenic low back pain concludes, according to the article's title, that fusion was effective and non-surgical treatment was not. We could not verify the sample size or the figures. The population is highly selected, and the result contradicts earlier trials. It does not justify broadening fusion indications.

The manufacturer of the Synergy cervical disc presented two-year data on one hundred seventy-five patients. They were compared with one hundred ninety-two historical controls after anterior cervical discectomy and fusion. Composite clinical success was eighty-seven point one versus fifty-six point six percent. The control group is historical and the data come from the company. This is device information, not proof of superiority.

A blood test based on six circulating microribonucleic acid molecules classifies progression risk in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis. It received Breakthrough Device designation from the United States Food and Drug Administration. The underlying study included one hundred sixteen patients, with one hundred percent sensitivity and specificity in an internal test set. Designation is not approval, and perfect figures in a small sample require independent external validation.

In Japan, Shinshu University reported that cold, more than snow, may be associated with proximal femoral fractures in older adults in Nagano Prefecture. The public page does not give the journal, sample size or figures. It is an epidemiological signal, not practice-changing data.

At the Orthopaedic Trauma Association meeting in Nashville, a company showed new plates and a Bluetooth-enabled fixator. It also showed a three-dimensional intraoperative guidance system assisted by artificial intelligence, expected in twenty twenty-seven. The announcement includes no clinical data or regulatory clearance dates. This is market information, not evidence.

Finally, in Ireland a commission of investigation was recommended into pediatric scoliosis, spina bifida and hip dysplasia care. It covers twenty nineteen to twenty twenty-five. This is a health policy decision. It is a reminder that delays in pediatric spine surgery are a patient safety issue.

Thank you for listening. Until next month.
