This is what is next in orthopedics and traumatology, with five milestones that have official dates between November and the end of April next year: two pending decisions from the United States Food and Drug Administration and three specialty meetings. Keep in mind that the agency's dates are review target dates, not approvals. We also looked at large trials in discogenic low back pain and in hip fracture, but their results either fall outside this window or have no public date, so we left them out.

First, from November fifth to eighth, the American Association of Hip and Knee Surgeons holds its annual meeting in the Dallas area of Texas. It is the reference forum for primary and revision arthroplasty and usually brings data on periprosthetic infection, outpatient surgery, analgesia, thromboprophylaxis and robotic surgery. The critical take: much of what is presented comes from retrospective or single-center series, often with industry involvement, so it is wise to wait for full publication before changing protocols.

Second, on February fourth, the agency is due to decide on infigratinib, an oral inhibitor of fibroblast growth factor receptors, for children with achondroplasia. In the phase three trial against placebo, annualized growth velocity rose by about one and three quarter centimeters per year more than with placebo after one year, with better age-adjusted height. Today the approved options, daily vosoritide and weekly navepegritide, are subcutaneous injections, so a pill would ease years of treatment. The critical take: the trial is company sponsored and measures an intermediate outcome; we still do not know whether it improves final adult height, reduces spine or limb surgery, or prevents foramen magnum compression, and cost will be a barrier in our setting.

Third, from March third to sixth, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons holds its annual meeting in Las Vegas, with the Saturday devoted to subspecialty societies. It is the specialty's main showcase and where the academy usually rolls out its clinical practice guidelines. The critical take: the volume of abstracts is huge and uneven in quality; what matters is what later appears in full.

Fourth, from April eighth to eleventh, Vienna hosts the world congress on osteoporosis, osteoarthritis and musculoskeletal diseases, organized by the International Osteoporosis Foundation and its European partner society. It matters for secondary prevention of fragility fractures, since most patients with a hip or vertebral fracture still leave hospital without osteoporosis treatment. The critical take: industry presence is strong, and many data are preliminary or hard to apply in lower-resource systems.

Fifth, on April twenty-fourth, a few days past our usual window, the agency is due to decide on a cryopreserved amniotic allograft given as a single knee injection for osteoarthritis pain. Of its two phase three trials against saline, the first was positive, but the second, with almost six hundred patients, missed its primary goal of pain reduction at six months, and the company supports its application with a pooled analysis of both. The need is real, because steroids and hyaluronic acid give modest or short-lived relief. The critical take: company sponsorship, one negative trial, a saline comparator that produces a strong placebo effect in osteoarthritis, and small differences; and approval would not validate the many amniotic tissue products already sold without trials.
