Good morning, colleagues. This is the monthly rheumatology bulletin from Boletines Médicos, covering September eighteenth to October eighteenth, twenty twenty-six. It was a transition month ahead of the American College of Rheumatology annual meeting. Here are seven verified stories.

We start with rheumatoid arthritis and a practical question. Can we safely cut the dose of a biologic or a Janus kinase inhibitor in a stable patient? A randomized, open-label noninferiority trial from South Korea enrolled three hundred forty-eight patients who had been in low disease activity for at least six months. One hundred seventy-one had their dose reduced by half in steps, and one hundred seventy-seven stayed on usual dosing. At twenty-four weeks, low disease activity was maintained in eighty-eight point two percent with tapering, versus eighty-nine point six percent on usual dosing, and the difference stayed within the noninferiority margin. The critical read: tapering looks reasonable and saves money, but twenty-four weeks of open-label follow-up says nothing about joint damage, and the authors themselves call for longer studies.

Next, polymyalgia rheumatica. On September eighteenth, the European Medicines Agency reported that its scientific committee recommended extending the indication of secukinumab, and Novartis confirmed the same day that the extension is for this disease. The basis is the Replenish trial, a phase three, double-blind, placebo-controlled study in twenty-seven countries, with a twenty-four-week steroid taper. According to Novartis, both doses met all endpoints and secukinumab doubled sustained remission at one year versus placebo, although the release gives no percentages. The critical read: this could become an option for steroid-dependent patients, but the final European decision is still pending, and the actual rates should be read in the published paper.

In Sjögren's disease, Amgen reported on September twenty-second that dazodalibep met its primary goal in the Oasis three-oh-one trial, a phase three, double-blind, placebo-controlled study. According to the company, about six hundred twenty-one adults with moderate to severe systemic disease took part. Disease activity improved significantly at forty-eight weeks, with improvement seen as early as week four. The most common side effects were nasopharyngitis, urinary tract infection, high blood pressure, and infusion reactions. The critical read: this is a company press release with no effect size, so we should wait for the full data.

In cutaneous lupus, Biogen presented one-year data for litifilimab in Vienna on October second. Among ninety-three patients refractory or intolerant to antimalarials, twenty-one point seven percent reached a seventy percent improvement in skin activity at twenty-four weeks with the drug, versus five point eight percent on placebo. At one year, among those treated from the start, the figure rose to twenty-eight point eight percent. The critical read: a small sample, no comparator after week twenty-four, and no peer review yet; phase three results are expected in the first half of twenty twenty-seven.

Turning to gout. A prospective study from Singapore followed one hundred fifty patients starting allopurinol escalation. One in five developed raised liver enzymes, almost always mild and never severe. Of nineteen patients with a rise at the second visit, eighteen kept or increased the dose, and in many of them the values stabilized, improved, or normalized by the next visit. The rise was linked to alcohol use and to not taking a statin, not to the allopurinol dose. The critical read: a small observational study in almost exclusively male patients, but it supports not stalling treat-to-target therapy over mild liver test changes.

In osteoarthritis, The Lancet published a global health policy roadmap on World Arthritis Day, October twelfth, written by experts from eighteen countries and led from Curtin University in Australia. About six hundred million people lived with osteoarthritis in twenty twenty-three, and nearly one billion will by twenty fifty. The disability burden from the disease rose sixty-five percent between nineteen ninety and twenty twenty-three. The critical read: a policy document, not a trial, but useful for putting osteoarthritis on national chronic disease agendas.

In targeted therapies, AbbVie announced on September twenty-second that the European Commission approved upadacitinib for polyarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis from age two, including a weight-based oral solution. In an open-label, single-arm study of one hundred twenty-two children, seventy-nine point five percent achieved a seventy percent pediatric improvement at forty-eight weeks. The critical read: there was no comparator, and long-term safety of this class in children remains unclear.

A note on transparency. This month we found no new verifiable data on osteoporosis, vasculitis, or cell therapy, and no regional data from Latin America and the Caribbean.

On the calendar. October twentieth is World Osteoporosis Day. An American regulatory decision on tildrakizumab for psoriatic arthritis is expected on October twenty-ninth. And the American College of Rheumatology meets in Orlando from November sixth to eleventh.

Thank you for listening. Until next month.
