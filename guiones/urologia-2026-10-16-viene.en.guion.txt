Good morning, colleagues. This edition of what's next in urology looks at the coming months, from now through March two thousand twenty-seven. There are five milestones with verifiable dates: two pending regulatory decisions in the United States and three meetings where new data are expected. None of this is established practice yet, and it should be read that way.

First, muscle-invasive bladder cancer in patients who cannot receive cisplatin or decline it. The United States Food and Drug Administration is giving priority review to perioperative durvalumab plus neoadjuvant enfortumab vedotin, based on the open-label phase three VOLGA trial with six hundred ninety-five randomized patients. According to AstraZeneca, the interim analysis significantly improved event-free survival and overall survival versus cystectomy alone, with or without adjuvant treatment. A decision is expected in the fourth quarter of two thousand twenty-six. Note that the company has not yet released hazard ratios, the triplet arm with tremelimumab did not reach significance for overall survival at that interim analysis, and an alternative has been approved in the United States since November twenty-first, two thousand twenty-five for cisplatin-ineligible patients, enfortumab with pembrolizumab. The real question will be which combination to choose, and at what cost.

Second, metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer with alterations in homologous recombination repair genes. The same agency is giving priority review to talazoparib plus enzalutamide, based on the randomized, double-blind phase three TALAPRO three trial, with five hundred ninety-nine patients who had no more than three months of prior androgen deprivation therapy. Pfizer reports a fifty-two percent reduction in the risk of radiographic progression or death versus enzalutamide plus placebo. A decision is expected before the end of two thousand twenty-six, and the European agency is also reviewing it. Critical take: overall survival is not yet mature and the company itself cautions it may not be met, and the primary endpoint is radiographic. It also requires genomic sequencing, which is still not routine in many of our centers.

Third, the annual meeting of the Society of Urologic Oncology, December second to fourth, two thousand twenty-six, in San Diego. This is where non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer data are often discussed, at a time of many new intravesical therapies following the shortage of bacillus Calmette-Guérin. The late-breaking program has not been published, so it is best not to anticipate specific results.

Fourth, the American Society of Clinical Oncology genitourinary cancers symposium, February eleventh to thirteenth, two thousand twenty-seven, in San Francisco. It is one of the usual showcases for phase three trials in prostate, bladder, and kidney cancer. Fuller data from the trials now under regulatory review could appear there, but that will depend on the companies and the meeting committees.

Fifth, the forty-second annual congress of the European Association of Urology, March nineteenth to twenty-second, two thousand twenty-seven, in Amsterdam. It is the reference meeting for European urology across all areas, not only oncology. Historically the European guidelines are updated around the congress, but for this edition the association has not yet announced a publication date, so we treat it as an expectation, not a fact.

In short: two United States decisions that could change systemic treatment in bladder and prostate cancer before year end, and three meetings to check whether the data hold up to what the press releases say. We will come back with results once peer-reviewed publications are available.

Thank you for listening.
